### The core story (this is the part that matters for the course, not just trivia)
Neural networks began as attempts to computationally model the brain/cognition, not as an engineering technique invented from scratch. The lineage:
- **Associationism** (Aristotle through Hume/Locke/Pavlov, ~400BC–1900s): learning = forming associations between things experienced together (contiguity, frequency, similarity, contrast).
- **Connectionism** (Hartley 1749 "vibratiuncles" as an early precursor; Alexander Bain 1873 explicitly: "the information is in the connections"): the brain's knowledge/processing lives in the *connections* between neurons, not in some separate storage — a "connectionist machine" is a network of simple processing units where all the "program" is encoded as connection weights, contrasted with a Von Neumann machine (separate program/processor/memory).
- **Turing's Connectionist Machines (1948)**: Turing proposed "unorganized machines" made of simple threshold units wired randomly, then *trained* into organized behavior by selectively disabling/enabling connections — an early, largely overlooked anticipation of learning-by-adjusting-connections. He described **A-type** unorganized machines (random networks of NAND-like elements, no training) and **B-type** machines (A-type machines augmented with "modifier" units on each connection that could be trained to interfere with or pass signals through — effectively a crude trainable-weight mechanism). This work predates Rosenblatt's perceptron by a decade but wasn't widely recognized until much later, since it was buried in an unpublished 1948 report. Because A-type machines are built from NAND-equivalent elements (a functionally complete gate), both A-type and B-type machines are Boolean-universal — they can compose arbitrary Boolean functions, same as an MCP network.
- **PDP requirements (Rumelhart, Hinton, McClelland 1986, "Parallel Distributed Processing")**: laid out a checklist for what makes a system properly "connectionist"/PDP in spirit — a set of simple processing units, connected by weighted links, each unit having an activation level, propagation/output rules for how activation flows and is emitted to neighbors, an activation rule for how a unit combines inputs into a new activation, and (crucially) a **learning rule** for how connection weights change with experience. The point of the checklist: it's not enough to have neuron-like units — you need this whole package (units + weighted connections + activation dynamics + a learning rule) before a system counts as genuinely connectionist/PDP.
- **Connectionist Systems requirements (Bechtel & Abrahamsen, 1991)**: a related but distinct philosophical framing of what counts as a connectionist system, emphasizing that knowledge is distributed across many weighted connections rather than stored in discrete symbolic representations, and that processing is inherently parallel across simple units rather than sequential. Framed as a contrast against classical/symbolic AI, not just a technical checklist like PDP's.
- **McCulloch & Pitts (1943)**: first formal mathematical neuron model — a neuron evaluates a Boolean proposition: weighted excitatory inputs summed, any inhibitory input firing forces output to 0. A *network* of MCP neurons (not a single one) can compute arbitrary Boolean functions in principle, but had **no learning rule**.
- **Hebb (1949)**: "neurons that fire together wire together" — `w = w + η·x·y`. First real learning rule, but fundamentally *unstable* (no mechanism to shrink weights, so strong connections just keep growing unbounded — this is the answer to Poll 2 below).
- **Rosenblatt's Perceptron (1958)**: a neuron with a **provably convergent learning rule** for linearly separable problems — `w = w + η(d(x) − y(x))x`, i.e. update weights by the error times the input, only when wrong. Big claims were made about it ("will walk, talk, see..." — NYT 1958), later shown to be overhyped. Note: a *single* perceptron is NOT Boolean-universal (can't do XOR) — universality only comes once you network multiple perceptrons together (the MLP fix below). This single-vs-network distinction is a recurring exam trap.
- **Minsky & Papert (1969)**: showed a *single* perceptron can't solve XOR — it can only draw one straight-line (linear) decision boundary, and XOR's two "1" points and two "0" points sit at opposite corners of a square, which no single line can separate. This nearly killed neural net research for years ("AI winter").
- **The fix, Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)**: stack a hidden layer of perceptrons, each drawing its own line, then combine their outputs in a further layer (e.g. XOR = OR of two ANDs). This is exactly the intuition we built together before this lecture even landed — a hidden layer lets you compose multiple linear cuts into a non-linear boundary. MLPs can represent **arbitrary Boolean functions**.

### From Boolean to real-valued (this is where it gets to what you'll actually code)
- Real-valued perceptron: `y = θ(w·x + b)` — a weighted sum plus bias (an *affine* function, not just linear — linear would mean no `b` term, affine allows the offset), passed through a threshold activation `θ(z) = 1 if z≥0 else 0`.
- Geometrically, one real-valued perceptron is still just a **linear classifier** — it cuts the input space with one straight line/hyperplane.
- Networks of these perceptrons can compose arbitrarily complex decision boundaries: each unit contributes one linear cut, and higher layers (AND/OR-like combinations) stitch the cuts into polygons of any shape. A hexagonal region needs 6 "edge" perceptrons + 1 combining perceptron = 7 total (see Poll 3).
- MLPs are **universal classifiers**: for any decision boundary, you can build an MLP that approximates it to arbitrary precision. (High-dimensional example given: MNIST digit classification as finding a boundary in 784-dimensional space.)
- MLPs can also do **regression** (continuous-valued outputs), not just classification: a small MLP can compose narrow "square pulses" that sum into an approximation of any function, and adding more units makes the pulses narrower/more precise — this is the seed of the **Universal Approximation** idea that's the entire topic of Lecture 2.
- MLPs can also model memory (via loops) and represent probability distributions over data — previews of RNNs and generative models later in the course.

### Poll questions (practice — try them yourself before reading the answer)
1. **Who first proposed connectionism?** → Alexander Bain (1873, "Mind and Body" — "the information is in the connections"). Not Aristotle (associationism, much older idea) or David Hartley (an earlier precursor, "vibratiuncles," but the deck frames Bain as the one who formally "enters" connectionism).
2. **Roughly how many connections between neurons in the human brain?** → 100 trillion. (Bain's own 1873 guess was ~1 million neurons / 5 billion connections — that's the outdated historical estimate, a trap answer. Modern estimate for neuron *count* is ~80–86 billion, but *connections/synapses* is roughly 100 trillion.)
3. **Hebbian learning is...** → Fundamentally unstable, since there is no reduction in weights (nothing counterbalances growth, so strong connections just keep strengthening unboundedly).
4. **How many perceptrons to model a hexagonal decision region?** → 7 (one linear "edge" unit per side of the hexagon = 6, plus one combining/AND unit in the next layer = 7). General pattern: N-sided polygon needs N+1 units.
5. **How many neurons (with sinusoidal activation y=sin(z)) to exactly model y=cos(2x)?** → "None of the above" — trick question, the real answer is exactly **1**, since cos(2x) = sin(2x + π/2), so a single neuron with weight 2 and bias π/2 reproduces it exactly. None of the numeric options (3, floor/ceil(π/2), infinite) equal 1.
6. **Which computational systems can compose arbitrary Boolean functions? (select all)** McCulloch-Pitts connectionist network / Turing B-type machine / a single Rosenblatt perceptron / a digital circuit of OR and NOT gates → **MCP network, Turing B-type, and OR+NOT circuit are all TRUE; a single perceptron is FALSE.** MCP networks and Turing's A/B-type machines are both built from functionally-complete gate-equivalents (able to represent any truth table via a network of units). {OR, NOT} is also functionally complete on its own — De Morgan's law gives you AND for free: `A AND B = NOT(NOT A OR NOT B)`. A single perceptron, by contrast, is one linear cut and provably cannot do XOR — universality requires a *network* of perceptrons (the MLP), not one unit. The pattern: Boolean universality is a property of *networks* of simple threshold units, never of a single unit alone.

## Lecture 2 (Aug 27, 2026): What Can a Network Represent

### The core story
This lecture formalizes the Lecture 1 teaser about depth. Headline result: **depth buys exponential savings in the number of neurons needed, for some functions, compared to a single wide hidden layer — not as an approximation tradeoff, as an exact-representation tradeoff.**

- **Formal depth/layers**: a network is a graph; sources = inputs (no incoming edges), sinks = outputs (no outgoing edges); depth = length of the longest source-to-sink path. "Deep" conventionally means 3+ layers (at least one hidden layer).
- **MLPs as universal Boolean functions**: AND/OR/NOT/majority gates are all buildable from single perceptrons (tune weights + bias). XOR needs exactly **3 perceptrons** (OR and NAND in layer 1, AND of those in layer 2) — the concrete fix to Minsky & Papert. Any Boolean function can be written in **DNF** (OR of ANDs, one AND per truth-table row where output=1) and mechanically converted into an MLP with **exactly one hidden layer**: the hidden layer holds one AND-of-literals perceptron per truth-table row where output=1 (negated variables are handled with negative weights inside that same perceptron — no separate NOT layer needed), and the single output-layer perceptron is the OR combining them. So a Boolean MLP needs only **one hidden layer (plus the output layer)** to be exactly universal — not two — though that hidden layer can need exponentially many neurons in the worst case. *(Correction from an earlier draft of these notes, which mis-described this as a 2-hidden-layer construction — the output OR-gate is the output layer, not a second hidden layer.)*
- **Karnaugh maps** reduce a raw DNF by merging adjacent same-output truth-table rows, shrinking the needed AND-gates for most real functions.
- **The checkerboard/parity worst case**: output = XOR of all N inputs has zero adjacent-mergeable cells — the "largest irreducible DNF." Needs ~**2^(N−1) hidden units** in one hidden layer, exactly.
- **Depth vs. width tradeoff, concretely**: parity of N variables costs **2^(N−1)+1 neurons in 1 hidden layer** (shallow) vs. **3(N−1) neurons across ~2·log2(N) layers** (deep, via cascaded pairwise XOR-of-3 units). Same exact function, exponential vs. linear neuron count.
- **Formal complexity-theory backing** (not just an MLP quirk): Shannon's theorem (most Boolean functions need exponential-size circuits); Furst/Saxe/Sipser 1984 (parity ∉ AC⁰ — provably no constant-depth polynomial-size circuit computes parity); threshold circuits (TC, what perceptrons are) are strictly more powerful per-gate than plain Boolean circuits (AC); analogous depth-efficiency theorems exist for arithmetic/sum-product circuits (Valiant; Bengio & Delalleau).
- **MLPs as universal classifiers (geometric view)**: N-sided polygon = N edge-perceptrons + 1 combiner = N+1 units (matches the hexagon poll from Lecture 1). Circles = polygon limit with infinitely many edges, bias ≈ −N/2 in the combiner. Arbitrary/disconnected shapes = OR together many circle-sub-networks. In the idealized limit of unlimited width, a single hidden layer (plus output) is theoretically sufficient for universal classification too — depth is never strictly *required* for representability, only for avoiding exponential blowup in neuron count. Same "one layer suffices in theory, depth is about efficiency" pattern as the Boolean case above.
- **Optimal depth / information bottleneck (subtle, important)**: a network's **first hidden layer** with fewer than the required number of **threshold-activation** neurons can **provably never** represent certain fine-grained patterns (e.g. a 16-region grid needs ≥16 first-layer units) — no amount of added depth downstream can recover information a threshold gate already destroyed, because threshold activation collapses input to a hard 0/1 bit. **Graded activations** (sigmoid, tanh, ReLU) leak partial ("how strongly," not just "did it") information through narrow layers, letting later layers partially recover what threshold gates would have irreversibly lost — first hint in the course of why real nets use ReLU/graded activations instead of hard thresholds.
- **Network capacity**: three equivalent-ish framings — VC dimension (largest point set the classifier can shatter), storage/information capacity (bits of arbitrary mapping it can memorize), and disconnected-convex-region count (ties back to the polygon/circle construction). For threshold/linear units, the classical VC-dimension bound (Baum & Haussler) is on the order of **W·log(W)**, where W = number of weights — NOT W² (a plausible-sounding but incorrect distractor to watch for). Flagged as preview material, precise treatment comes later in the course.

### Check-in / practice questions (for self-testing later)
1. **Q:** Build an MLP for parity (XOR) of 8 boolean inputs using the deep cascade construction — how many total perceptrons, how many layers?
   **A:** 3(N−1) = 3(7) = **21 perceptrons**, arranged in roughly 2·log2(8) = **6 layers** (pairing inputs in a binary-tree fashion rather than a flat linear chain gets you closer to the log-depth bound).
2. **Q (multi-select):** Which are true about MLPs? (a) exactly one hidden layer + output is a Universal Boolean Machine; (b) VC dimension of a threshold-unit MLP is bounded by the square of the number of weights; (c) even in theory, MLPs may need more than one hidden layer for complex decision boundaries; (d) deeper networks generally require far fewer neurons than shallower networks for the same function.
   **A:** **(a) and (d) are TRUE; (b) and (c) are FALSE.** (b) is false because the real bound is O(W log W), not W². (c) is false because in the idealized/unlimited-width theoretical setting, one hidden layer suffices for universal classification (same pattern as (a)) — depth is about neuron-count efficiency, not raw representability.
